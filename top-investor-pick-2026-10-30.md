## 1. The Ticker & Business Overview — *what they do, and why Wall Street ignores them*

**What they do:** IA designs and builds the "glass cockpit" — flat-panel flight displays, air-data computers, autothrottle/autoland systems, flight management systems, and standby instruments. Its sweet spot is the **retrofit and aftermarket**: taking aging commercial, military, and business-aviation fleets and modernizing their avionics at a fraction of a full OEM program's cost. Customers span Boeing-platform operators, the U.S. military (e.g. tanker/transport fleets), Pilatus, Textron/King Air, and others. Founded in 1988 by **Geoffrey S.M. Hedrick** (a serial avionics entrepreneur who still chairs the board and is a large shareholder); run day-to-day by CEO Shahram Askarpour.

**Why Wall Street ignores them — this is the "overlooked zone" in its purest form:**
- **No analyst coverage at all.** The live ratings feed returns nothing — not a single sell-side analyst publishes on it. There is no institutional narrative to price the story in.
- **Micro-cap with a tiny float.** ~$371M cap, only ~17M shares trade, and insiders own 36% — far too small and too illiquid for most funds to touch.
- **"Boring" and invisible.** 147 employees, a sleepy Pennsylvania suburb, and a product category (cockpit retrofit avionics) that generates zero headlines in an era obsessed with AI mega-caps.
- It is, in short, exactly the kind of small, high-quality industrial that gets discovered *after* the fact.

## 3. The Competitive Moat — *why bigger players can't just take the niche*

- **Certification is the moat.** Every avionics product must earn FAA/EASA Supplemental Type Certificates (STCs) and military qualifications — a slow, costly, platform-by-platform process. Once IA is certified and often **sole-sourced** on a platform, switching costs for the operator are enormous and new entrants are effectively walled out for years.
- **A high-margin installed base.** Each unit sold drags along decades of recurring spares and service revenue tied to the life of the aircraft.
- **"Too small for the primes, too hard for newcomers."** IA's growth engine is *buying mature avionics product lines from Honeywell and Collins* — autopilot and generator lines the giants consider immaterial — and then running them at far higher margins because the fixed-cost base is tiny. It occupies the exact gap that is beneath a prime's attention yet above a startup's certification reach.
- **Founder alignment.** With insiders owning 36% (founder still chairman), management is spending its own money — incentives are pointed squarely at long-term per-share value.

## 4. The Tech & AI / Automation Advantage — *using tech, not selling the hype*

IA is not an "AI company" — it is a company whose *entire edge is applying modern software-defined avionics and a capital-light operating model* to an old-economy niche:
- Its products **are** cockpit automation — digital flat-panel displays and autoflight systems replacing legacy analog instruments.
- It runs an **asset-light, outsourced-manufacturing** model with a 147-person team, which is how a company this small posts ~$573K revenue/employee and ~48% gross margins. It then **digitizes and re-platforms acquired legacy product lines**, stripping out the primes' overhead. The efficiency *is* the moat-widener: every acquired line gets more profitable under IA than it was under its former owner.

## 5. The Specific Growth Trigger — *the upcoming catalyst*

**The near-term catalyst: FY2026 full-year earnings, expected ~December 17, 2026 (tentative).**

Why this specific report matters:
- It is the **first full fiscal year** to reflect the recently acquired **Honeywell autopilot ($22M) and generator ($8M) product lines plus S-TEC ($3.5M)** — a step-change in revenue scale (FY25 was $84M; FY26 is tracking materially higher on the nine-month run-rate).
- IA has a **chronic beat pattern**: it has topped EPS estimates in **5 of the last 6 quarters**, several times by 2–3x (e.g. FY25 Q4 actual $0.39 vs. $0.13 estimate). The consensus pencil for this report (~$0.27) looks beatable on form.
- A **backlog of ~$83–87M** (roughly a full year of revenue, and *excluding* potential sole-source production orders) gives unusual visibility for a micro-cap.

**The next leg — the real prize:** The **Aydin Displays acquisition closed in July 2026** — IA's *first purchase of an entire operating business* (ruggedized military/defense displays), not just a product line. FY2026 captures only about a quarter of it; **FY2027 is the first full year of Aydin**, so the December report's **forward guidance** is the moment the market gets its first clean look at the combined entity's earning power. If/when the company scales past the threshold where a first analyst initiates coverage or it enters small-cap indices, a re-rating of a no-coverage, 20x-earnings micro-cap becomes the structural catalyst beyond the quarter.

## Risks to size the position around (be honest)
- **Micro-cap illiquidity** — ~17M-share float; entries/exits move the price, and the stock has already round-tripped from $8 (Nov '25) to $31 (Mar '26) back to ~$21.
- **Acquisition-driven growth** carries integration and customer-retention risk, and added the modest debt noted above.
- **Lumpy quarters & customer concentration** are normal in defense/retrofit avionics.
- **No analyst coverage cuts both ways** — it's why the opportunity exists, but it also means no external estimates to anchor sentiment and thinner information flow.
